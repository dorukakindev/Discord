# Nicolas Flamel
> **Gelenek:** Alchemy · **Major works:** Livre des figures hiéroglyphiques (Book of Hieroglyphic Figures) · **Tür:** kisi · **Mitoloji:** Okültistler
*14th century French alchemist, scribe and manuscript-seller, legendary for his alleged discovery of the Philosopher's Stone and achievement of the Great Work (Grand Œuvre)*

![gorsel](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/73/Nicolas_Flamel_Histoire_critique.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=api&utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled)

Nicolas Flamel was a historical figure of the 14th century who became one of the most famous names in alchemical legend. According to the sources, he lived in Paris where he worked as a scribe and manuscript-seller. The key biographical source is the work by Albert Poisson titled "L'alchimie au XIVème siècle: Nicolas Flamel, Sa vie – ses fondations – ses œuvres" (Paris: Bibliothèque Chacornac, 1893; reprinted Paris: Gutenberg, 1981), which was followed by a reimpression of the "Livre des figures hiéroglyphiques" (Book of Hieroglyphic Figures). Poisson also published an earlier related work "Théories et symboles des alchimistes: Le Grand-Œuvre suivi d'un essai sur la bibliographie alchimique du XIXème siècle" (Paris: Bibliothèque Chacornac, 1891; Paris: Éditions Traditionnelles, 1969). Flamel's legendary fame rests on his alleged discovery of the Philosopher's Stone and his successful completion of the Great Work (Grand Œuvre), the ultimate goal of alchemy—the transmutation of base metals into gold and the attainment of immortality through the elixir of life. The "Livre des figures hiéroglyphiques" became the central text attributed to him, presenting alchemical secrets through symbolic imagery. His figure represents the archetype of the successful alchemical adept who achieved both spiritual and material transmutation. The sources indicate that Flamel's legend was particularly significant for 19th and early 20th century alchemical revival, as evidenced by Poisson's scholarly efforts to document his life and works. The reference to "ses fondations" in Poisson's title suggests Flamel was also associated with charitable foundations or endowed institutions, a common motif in legends of successful alchemists who used their transmuted gold for pious purposes.
