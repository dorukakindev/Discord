*A trademarked spirit board used to contact and communicate with spirits, consisting of a flat board printed with letters, numbers, words, and symbols, and a movable pointer (planchette) that rolls over the board to spell out messages in response to questions.*

Ouija boards are a type of spirit board, also known as witch board or talking board. The name "Ouija" is trademarked and currently marketed by Parker Brothers game and toy manufacturers, though it is often used generically to refer to any spirit board. The standard modern spirit board is a flat board printed with letters, numbers, words, and symbols, with a movable pointer that rolls over the board and points to printed material in response to questions.

Spirit boards originally came into prominence in the nineteenth century as tools associated with Spiritualism, enabling individuals or small groups to contact spirits independently without needing a séance or medium. They were sold as novelty items as well as serious spiritual tools. Although these devices first achieved mass popularity in the nineteenth century, they are based on ancient divinatory techniques. Chinese variations known as spirit or sand writing have been documented at least as far back as the Tang era (618–907 CE). The method involves writing a question on paper, burning it, and using a stylus suspended over a shallow tray filled with sand, operated by mediums until characters or words may be deciphered.

The Ouija board has had a turbulent history. In 1920, William Fuld tried to avoid paying taxes on the grounds that the Ouija was a scientific device for mediumship and therefore qualified for religious status tax exemption. The federal government considered it a "sporting game." A federal court ruled in favor of the government, and the U.S. Supreme Court upheld this ruling in 1922. The Ouija has been legally classified as a game or toy ever since.

By the late 1920s, the Ouija fad was in decline. In 1927, William Fuld fell three stories to his death from the roof of his company at age 54. His company was taken over by his sons, William and Hubert. In 1966, Parker Brothers purchased the trademark and rights.

The Ouija achieved renewed popularity during and after World War I, as thousands of bereaved persons attempted to contact the spirits of soldiers killed in the war. It became popular again in the 1960s.

Notable creative works inspired by Ouija use include: Pulitzer Prize-winning poet James Merrill composed his epic poem "The Changing Light at Sandover" from two decades of spirit communications via the Ouija. In 1913, Pearl Curran, a St. Louis homemaker, began receiving communications from "Patience Worth," a 16th-century English woman, via the Ouija; Worth dictated an astounding volume of poetry and novels in an old English dialect. In 1919, Betty and Stewart Edward White were using a Ouija as a lark when Betty was instructed to "get a pencil" and take dictation, beginning a long relationship with the "Invisibles" who dispensed metaphysical wisdom, compiled into "The Betty Book." In 1963, Jane Roberts was using the Ouija when she met "Seth," who purported to be a highly developed entity and dictated highly regarded books on metaphysics; Roberts began anticipating the Ouija board's answers in her head until she felt impelled to speak them aloud, developing into full trance channeling sessions. The New Age author Ruth Montgomery began her channeling with a Ouija then moved to automatic typing.

The Ouija board can be observed in action in director Roland West's 1930 film "The Bat Whispers" (among the inspirations for the modern superhero Batman).

Spirit boards have accrued something of a bad reputation, possibly because they are commonly operated by those with little or no metaphysical or spiritual training, described as "the equivalent of children playing with matches." Despite being sold in toy stores, they are considered real, genuine divinatory devices.

There are two theories as to the sources of answers derived from a Ouija board: one holds that the subconscious prompts the hands to spell out desired answers; the second holds that real entities are contacted, many of which are negative, earthbound spirits who have not accepted their deaths and are bent on possessing living human beings. A person who uses a Ouija invites entities to communicate, providing an opening through which evil beings may gain a foothold.

Problems attributed to Ouija use include: people claiming to be instructed to commit crimes—even murder—via the Ouija; sexual assaults, beatings, torment, obsession, and possession blamed on spirit contacts initiated via Ouija boards. At first, users think they are talking with dead people they know or friendly spirits; after benign beginnings, communications turn dark and threatening as malicious and evil demons reveal their true selves, engaging in vicious attacks such as physical injury, rape, nightmares, visions of monsters, poltergeist-like disturbances, and even levitation of victims. Some victims must seek psychiatric help. Some psychiatrists and psychologists attribute this to material dredged up from the subconscious rather than actual entities.

Aleister Crowley was critical of open-ended Ouija use, stating: "When you use the Ouija board, you give permission for any unknown spirit to communicate with you. Would you open the front door to your house and let in anybody who felt like it? Of course not."

Demonologists Ed and Lorraine Warren called the Ouija "a notorious passkey to terror, even when the intent of communication is decidedly positive in nature." Of thousands of demonic and negative cases they investigated, they said four of ten involved problems resulting from the Ouija. Ed Warren stated that all doors to the supernatural—the Ouija, séances, conjuring and candle rituals, and automatic writing devices—"lead down a road of misfortune, terror, and ruin."

One of the Warrens' most sensational cases involved a Ouija board: in March 1974, they were consulted by the Donovan family, whose daughter Patty had been using a Ouija board for months to talk to a spirit who said he had died as a teenager. Patty asked the spirit to manifest, and the household began experiencing unpleasant phenomena including destruction, rains of rocks, levitation of objects, manifestation of black forms, and noises. The case was turned over to Catholic clergy for exorcism; a successful rite was performed on May 2, 1974, during which a demon manifested as a seven-foot-tall being with horns, cloven feet, and a tail, according to the Warrens.

Responsible use guidelines suggested by paranormal investigators and occultists include: not using the board for entertainment or open calls to the spirit world; not using alcohol or drugs prior to or during sessions; never using boards to contact or conjure demonic entities; asking for specific spirits by name rather than general calls; being clear and specific when working with spirits. The danger is that spirits sitting around with nothing to do tend to be ones no one else has called for, probably for good reason, and fear attracts the fearsome.

### Köken
The modern Ouija board originated in the United States in the late 19th century. The name was coined by combining the German words for "yes," oui and ja. The forerunner was the planchette (French for "little board"), a triangular or heart-shaped pointer on three legs that appeared in Europe in 1853, attributed to a French spiritualist named M. Planchette. In 1890, Elijah Bond and Charles Kennard founded the Kennard Novelty Company in Baltimore, Maryland to manufacture the Ouija board. William Fuld took over the company in 1901, renaming it the Ouija Novelty Company. In 1966, Parker Brothers of Salem, Massachusetts purchased the trademark and rights and markets the Ouija as entertainment.

### Tezahür
A flat board (or other flat surface) printed with letters, numbers, words, and symbols. A movable pointer (planchette) rolls over the board. The planchette is typically triangular or heart-shaped on three legs, sometimes with a clear plastic window added (as in William Fuld's 1910 modification).
