# Leviathan
### King of the Proud, King of all the Children of Pride
> **Köken:** Ancient Hebrew lore, Ugaritic mythology, medieval demonology · **Personality:** Especially mean-natured, even for a demon; symbol of chaos; King of the Proud; ruler of Envy among the Seven Deadly Sins; in later apocalyptic traditions aims to destroy the earth · **Element:** Water, Sea · **Tür:** demon
*Primordial sea monster demon of chaos, originally a female aquatic arch-she-demon created on the fifth day of Creation, later developed into male and female aspects in Hebrew lore, and ruler of Envy among the Seven Deadly Sins in medieval demonology.*

![gorsel](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9d/Destruction_of_Leviathan.png/960px-Destruction_of_Leviathan.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=api&utm_content=thumbnail)

In ancient Hebrew lore and popularized in medieval demonology, Leviathan was an aquatic, arch she-demon; she was also said to be a fallen angel of the Order of Seraphim. Her name in Hebrew means 'the crooked (or piercing) serpent (or dragon)' or 'whale.' Created by God on the fifth day of Creation, Leviathan is described as a monstrous female sea creature three hundred miles long with eyes glowing as brightly as twin suns. She is the symbol of chaos. Using her supernatural strength, she hunted and ate a whale a day. Her breath was so foul that to breathe it in was enough to kill. She could send a wave of intense heat from her mouth that could boil water instantly. Especially mean-natured, even for a demon, this demon was fearful of a species of sea-worm called a kilbit, as it clings to the gills. She was said to have lived in the Mediterranean Sea, but God slew the female leviathan, salted it, and fed it to His people so that it could not reproduce with its mate. The hide of the beast was used to make the tent the feast was held under. The male species of this demon is known as Behemoth. In Hebrew lore, Leviathan has two aspects, male—Leviathon, the Slant Serpent—and female—Lilith, the Tortuous Serpent. Leviathan was one of the possessing demons named in the Loudun Possessions. He is ruler of Envy, the fourth of the Seven Deadly Sins. In the Bible, Leviathan is mentioned in Job 41, Psalm 74:14, Psalm 104:26, and Isaiah 27:1. The word 'leviathan' in Hebrew means approximately 'that which gathers itself into folds' or 'that which is drawn out.' First attested in a Ugaritic text (KTU 1.5 i: 1 II 27) where it occurs as ltn, the name is related to a root LWY. Etymologically it might be interpreted either as 'the twisting one' or 'the wreath-like', 'the circular', both possibilities pointing to an original concept of Leviathan as a snake-being. In Ugaritic texts, Leviathan

is designated a 'fugitive serpent' (bln brl), smitten by the victorious weather-god Baalu. Two closely related epithets, 'wriggling serpent' (bln Cqltn) and 'Mighty one(?) with the seven heads' (Sly! d.sbCt rasm), are usually understood to refer to Leviathan too. According to 1 Enoch 60:7-9, 24, Leviathan is a female dragon located at the bottom of the sea above the sources, while Behemoth is a male dragon living in the desert; both will be prepared for the meal of the righteous at the eschatological banquet, an opinion shared by 2 Apoc. Bar. 29:4 and the Rabbis. In Apoc. Ahr. 21:4, Leviathan still appears as a monster having the sea as his domain and aiming to destroy the earth. The post-biblical career of Leviathan developed in two directions: one naturalistic and de-mythologizing, identified him with a whale (ketos, as LXX Job 3:8); the other, apocalyptic and more influential, continued to consider him a dragon (Heb tanyin, Aram talya or Gk drakon, as LXX Job 40:25). In Job 40:25-41:26, the second part of Yahweh's second answer to Job is entirely devoted to Leviathan. While the rhetorical questions of the first section (40:25-41:1) insist on Job's inability to capture him, the second (41:2-26) gives a panegyric description imbued with numinous fear. This text describes features of a crocodile, as recognized in 1663 by S. Bochart. But the crocodile-Leviathan, 'king of all beasts(?)' (41:26), is not simply considered as a zoological species. In Egyptian iconography, the crocodile appears as an enemy of the sun-god and is subdued by the god Horus or the Pharaoh. The author of Job 41 had access to some animal mythological literature relating to the Egyptian tradition. However, in contrast to this and to the Syrian tradition taken over by apocalypticists, he does not present his issue in terms of a mythological combat: Yahweh's own words are full of respect for the croc

odile-Leviathan; the latter, just as Behemoth, represents a symbolic residue, within reality, of evil and chaos which even the creator cannot expel beyond the boundaries of his creation. In Psalm 104:26, Leviathan is considered to be a harmless player in the sea. The Leviathan of the political and social philosophical system of Thomas Hobbes (1588-1679), in his The Leviathan, or the Matter, Form, and Power of a Commonwealth, etc. (1651), is only demonic in an allegorical sense, Hobbes making use of the supposed nature of the demon as King of the Proud. This Leviathan is essentially sovereign power, or the commonwealth as a political organism. The Leviathan of the poet Blake is a coiled sea serpent, a 'crooked serpent', a symbol of the warring evil in man. Monstrous though they are, Leviathan and Behemoth are said by Blake to be 'erecting pillars in the deepest Hell to reach the heavenly arches.' Blavatsky sees 'Leviathan' as a term formerly used in initiation, 'the Mystery of the Serpent of the Great Sea.' She says that esoterically Leviathan represents 'Deity in its double manifestation of good and evil.'

### Tezahür
Monstrous female sea creature three hundred miles long with eyes glowing as brightly as twin suns; in Job 41:15-17 described as having scales so closely set that no air may pass between them; in some traditions a coiled sea serpent or crooked serpent; seven-headed serpent in some Ugaritic references; crocodile-like features in Job 41; female dragon in 1 Enoch
