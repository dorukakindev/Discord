# Spiritualism
> **Bölge:** Originated in mid-19th century America (Upstate New York), spread throughout the Northeast United States, then to England, France, Germany, Italy, Spain, Brazil, and other countries worldwide · **Dönem:** Mid-19th century (1848) to present; peak period 1850s-1870s; transformed and incorporated occult elements by early 20th century
*A religious and philosophical movement based on the belief that living humans can communicate with spirits of the dead, usually through an intermediary medium. Originated with the Fox sisters' rappings in 1848 and spread rapidly as both a demonstration of immortality and a vehicle for social reform.*

In its most basic form, Spiritualism is the belief that living men can and do hold intercourse, usually through an intermediary, with 'spirits' of the dead. It is both a recurring possibility of human experience in all ages and a specific, historically defined phenomenon with roots in mid-19th century America. The movement originated in 1848 in Hydesville, New York, with the experiences of the three daughters of John D. Fox—Margaretta, Catherine (Kate), and their older sister Ann Leah Underhill. The family heard mysterious knockings that the children learned to communicate with through a system of coded raps. The first public demonstration was held at Corinthian Hall in Rochester on November 14, 1848. By the end of 1849, the movement was the subject of universal discussion throughout the Northeast and as far west as Ohio. By 1855, the number of spiritualists in the United States was estimated between one and eleven million in a country of only 28 million. The American Civil War (1861-1865) and revelations of fraudulent mediums put an end to rapid growth. Early Spiritualism lacked a coherent philosophy distinguished from non-spiritualists; each person was their own interpreter. The fundamental tenets were survival of human individuality after death and communication from the afterlife. The original message was simply proof of immortality through wondrous rappings. Early spiritualists were profoundly this-worldly reformers, advocating for 'Fatherhood of God and Brotherhood of Man,' free speech, free press, and equal rights. Almost all mid-century reformers (women's rights, abolition, prohibition, economic reform) dabbled in Spiritualism. The movement adopted Andrew Jackson Davis's 'Harmonial Philosophy' as its cosmological framework, viewing the cosmos as successive concentric universes thrown off from divine central fire, with souls progressing through ever higher sphe

res after death. Early Spiritualism rejected hidden wisdom, prisca theologia, spiritual elites, initiation, and secret societies as undemocratic. The medium was originally conceived merely as a 'wire' of the spiritual telegraph, bridging two worlds without special spiritual authority. By the mid-1850s, professional 'test mediums' emerged, and the movement turned to increasingly phenomenal, theatrical methods: automatic writing, light trance, full trance, painting mediums, slate readers, musical mediums, table-turning, and by the early 1870s, materialization of spirits. This led to widespread fraud and declining credibility. A profound crisis and transformation occurred in the early 1870s through the influence of Paschal Beverly Randolph, Emma Hardinge Britten, H.P. Blavatsky, and H.S. Olcott. Randolph introduced active magical practice, clairvoyance through magic mirrors, sexual magic, and the concept of humans elevating themselves to roles in celestial hierarchies through Imperial Will. Emma Hardinge Britten, an inspirational medium who came to New York in 1855, developed unorthodox Spiritualism through her Orphic Circle experience in London, teaching that embodied human spirits could produce all phenomena attributed to disembodied spirits through correct knowledge and strong will. She described a universe populated by gods, powers, and demigods beyond spirits of the dead, with humans able to become 'God-men—heavenly men, strong and mighty Powers, Thrones, Dominions, World-Builders, glorious hierarchies of Sun-bright Souls... Gods in person.' Blavatsky and Olcott attacked Spiritualism's fundamental tenet of the identity of communicating spirits, arguing that seance entities were discarded psychic shells rather than immortal spirits. By the early 20th century, Spiritualism had incorporated active will, individual spiritual development, magical practice, hierarchies of

beings, and reincarnation from Theosophy, forming a new koine. In England, Spiritualism developed organizational structures: the Spiritualists National Federation (1891, now Spiritualists National Union) and the London Spiritualists Alliance (later College of Psychic Sciences). The Society for Psychical Research was founded in 1883 for scientific study. In France, Spiritualism complicated by existing Mesmerism; Allan Kardec (Hypolyte Léon Denizard Rivail) developed 'spiritism' with doctrinal teachings from magnetized somnambules, including reincarnation on earth, distinguishing it from Anglo-American Spiritualism. His Le Livre des Esprits (1857) became the Bible of French spiritism. In Germany, Spiritualism found broader acceptance in intellectual and scientific circles than elsewhere, with Johann Karl Friedrich Zöllner and Gustav Theodor Fechner studying medium Henry Slade. The journal Psychische Studien (1874-1925) was founded by Russian Aleksandr N. Aksakov due to censorship in Russia. In Italy, Spiritualism developed along both spiritualist and Kardecist lines after D.D. Home's 1854 visit. In Spain, Kardec's books set the tone from the late 1850s; the 1868 revolution freed the movement from Catholic censorship. In Brazil, Kardecist Spiritualism arrived by the early 1870s, becoming perhaps the most active form worldwide, with two distinct paths: 'pure' Kardecist spiritism among wealthy Europeans, and a mixed form intermingling with Umbanda and Candomblé. By World War I, Spiritualism worldwide had incorporated western occultism, Theosophy, and New Thought, and in America adopted the outward form of a peripheral Christian church. At present it is distinguished from the New Age generally only by its emphasis on mediums and the seance.

### İlahlar
Early Spiritualism had limited role for non-human spirits; angels and great spiritual teachers (prophets, masters, mahatmas, adepts) played little role at early seances. Later transformed Spiritualism incorporated hierarchies of beings: gods, powers, demigods, celestial hierarchies including Thrones, Dominions, World-Builders. Kardecist spiritism emphasized a personal God.

Cahagnet, The Celestial Telegraph (1848); various spiritualist journals including The Spiritual Philosopher (1850), The Yorkshire Spiritual Telegraph and British Harmonial Advocate (1855-1859), The London Spiritual Magazine (1860-1877), The Spiritualist Newspaper (1869-1882), Revue Spirite (1858-), Revue Spiritualiste (1855-1870), Psychische Studien (1874-1925), The Banner of Light, The Religio-Philosophical Journal

### Pratikler
Seances with rapping spirits; communication through alphabet-number code; automatic writing; light trance and full trance mediumship; painting mediums; slate reading; musical mediums with instruments played by spirits; table-turning and furniture movement; materialization of spirits through cabinet apertures; public demonstrations by test mediums; preparatory singing of church hymns, dimming lights, holding hands around table; later incorporation of active clairvoyance, magic mirrors, sexual magic, and magical practice

### Kollar
American Spiritualism (original rapping-based, later reformed); British Spiritualism (organizational and analytical emphasis); French Spiritism/Kardecism (doctrinal, with reincarnation); German Spiritualism (intellectual/scientific acceptance); Italian Spiritualism (spiritualist and Kardecist lines); Spanish Spiritualism (Kardecist); Brazilian Spiritualism (pure Kardecist spiritism vs. mixed form with Umbanda and Candomblé); transformed Spiritualism incorporating Theosophy and occultism
