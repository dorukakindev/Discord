# Mephistopheles
### One of the Seven Great Princes of Hell, Prince of the Aerial Powers, Lord of Lightning and Thunder in Hell, Prince of Air and Pestilence, Chief of the Aerial Powers
> **Rütbe:** Prince · **Köken:** Faustbuch (1587), German literary demonology, medieval occultism, Faust legend · **Personality:** Trickster and practical joker; malicious, restless, stormy; dark through and through; shrewd negotiator; serves the lusts and desires of those with whom he contracts; ultimately seeks to trap souls into damnation · **Element:** Air · **Tür:** demon
*A literary demon originally created by the anonymous author of the Faustbuch (1587), Mephistopheles became incorporated into medieval demonological hierarchies as one of the seven princes of Hell and the demonic representative of the Devil in the Faust legend, serving as a trickster, tempter, and soul-collector through contractual pacts.*

Mephistopheles is purely a fictional creation by the anonymous author of the Faustbuch, published in 1587, and has ever since been associated as the Devil. The name has its roots in Greek and Hebrew and means 'destroyer liar' or 'he who loves not the light.' Although this demon is the fictional creation of an author, it did not stop medieval scholars and demonologists from adding him to their hierarchies. Medieval literature says that this fictional demon is one of the Princes of Hell, one of the Fallen Angels. The name was known to the German occultist Johannes Trithemius (1442–1516), who described him as 'a mysterious kind of demon, dark through and through, malicious, restless, stormy.' 'Mephistophiles' appears in 1527 in a Renaissance magical text, Praxis Magia Faustiana, and later as 'Mephostophiles' in the Faust chapbook Historia von D. Johann Fausten, first published in 1587 by an anonymous author. The chapbook tells the story of Dr. Johann Georg Faust, who bargains his soul to the Devil. Mephistophiles is invisible to others but can be seen by Faust in various shape-shifted guises, including as a grey friar monk. Fausts Hollenzwang describes 'Mephistophiel' as one of the seven great princes of Hell, who 'stands under the planet Jupiter, his regent is named Zadkiel, an enthroned angel of the holy Jehovah... his form is firstly that of a fiery bear, the other and fairer appearance is as of a little man with a black cape and a bald head.' The demon also manifests as an invisible ringing bell. Mephistopheles is a trickster and practical joker, who serves the lusts and desires of Faust. In the end, he is a shrewd negotiator who has the last laugh by trapping Faust into damnation. In some accounts, however, Faust manages to redeem himself and escape eternal punishment in hell. The origin of the name Mephistophiles is uncertain. The name (said to be constructed in la

te-medieval times from a symbolic trinity of Greek terms meaning 'not loving light') was used to denote demons connected with the Faust legend. Steiner says that Mephistopheles is the same as Ahriman. In Goethe's Faust, Mephistopheles pits against the philosophers the popular theory of the rugged remnants of chaos in nature, and the obstacles before which man is powerless. Shakespeare mentioned Mephistophilus in The Merry Wives of Windsor. The Faust theme has inspired many works of literature and musical compositions.
